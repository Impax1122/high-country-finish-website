Dust Control for Sign Installation in Hospitals and Occupied Buildings

Published September 26, 2026 · High Country Finish and Repair CO · Commercial Sign Installation

Most sign and photo-panel installs come down to a few dozen holes in a wall. In an empty building that dust is a cleanup chore. In a hospital, a clinic, an occupied office or a school, it is a health, safety and housekeeping problem, and the building has every right to hold the installer to a plan. Here is why dust control matters, which rules apply, and the capture methods we use when we drill in occupied spaces around Denver.

Why a Few Holes Matter

A single 1/4 inch hole in painted brick or block produces a small cone of powder, but a gallery wall with thirty standoff-mounted panels needs well over a hundred holes, and a rotary hammer throws the finest part of that dust into the air where it stays suspended and drifts. What the dust is made of is the real issue:

  • Crystalline silica. Brick, concrete, block, mortar and stone all contain it. Respirable silica is a known cause of silicosis and lung cancer, and OSHA regulates it for construction work at very low levels.
  • Mold spores and settled debris. Opening a wall or disturbing a ceiling releases whatever has been sitting there for decades. In healthcare, airborne fungal spores such as Aspergillus are a documented infection risk for immunocompromised patients, which is why hospitals run construction work through their infection prevention team.
  • Older materials. Buildings from before the 1980s can have lead in the paint and asbestos in some plasters, mastics and wall systems. If the building has not been surveyed, ask facilities before drilling into anything suspect.
  • Equipment and finishes. Fine dust gets into smoke detectors, return air grilles, electronics and freshly cleaned floors. A nuisance alarm or a lobby that has to be recleaned is an expensive way to hang signs.

The Rules That Apply

OSHA's Silica Standard for Construction

OSHA's respirable crystalline silica standard for construction (29 CFR 1926.1153) sets a permissible exposure limit of 50 micrograms per cubic meter over an eight-hour day. Most installers comply through Table 1 of the standard, which lists specific control methods for common tasks. For handheld drills and rotary hammers in masonry, Table 1 calls for:

  • A drill fitted with a commercially available shroud or cowling connected to a dust collection system.
  • A dust collector that provides the airflow the tool manufacturer recommends and has a filter that is 99 percent efficient or better, with a filter-cleaning mechanism.
  • A HEPA-filtered vacuum to clean out the holes, instead of blowing them out.

The standard also bans dry sweeping and brushing where it would contribute to exposure, and it bans cleaning clothing or surfaces with compressed air. In other words, the old habit of blowing the hole clean and sweeping the pile into a dustpan is not just messy, it is out of compliance.

Infection Control Risk Assessments in Healthcare

Hospitals, surgery centers and many clinics require an Infection Control Risk Assessment (ICRA) before any work that makes dust. The facility's infection prevention team rates the job by type of work and by the patient population in the area, then assigns a precaution class that spells out what the contractor must do. Short, low-dust tasks like drilling for signage often land in the lower classes, where the requirements look like this:

  • Work by methods that minimize dust, with dust captured at the tool.
  • Keep the work area contained and clean up with a HEPA vacuum and damp wipes, not a broom.
  • Use a tacky mat at the edge of the work zone, bag debris before transporting it, and keep doors to adjacent areas closed.
  • In higher classes, add barriers, sealed or isolated HVAC returns, and negative air with HEPA filtration.

The same wall can be rated differently depending on what is on the other side of it. A lobby corridor and a hallway outside an oncology unit are not the same job, and the facility, not the installer, makes that call. Ask for the ICRA permit before you schedule, and post it at the work area.

Capture Methods, From Best to Last Resort

The principle is simple: catch the dust at the bit, before it becomes airborne. Everything after that is cleanup.

MethodHow it worksWhere it fits
Drill shroud plus HEPA extractorA cowling around the bit connected by hose to a HEPA dust extractor that pulls dust away as it is createdMasonry, concrete and block; the default in healthcare and the Table 1 method under OSHA
Integrated tool dust extractionA rotary hammer with a built-in or clip-on extraction module and its own filterOverhead drilling and quick moves between many holes where a hose gets in the way
Hollow vacuum bitsA drill bit with a hollow core that pulls dust through the bit into the extractorAnchor holes in concrete where the hole also has to be clean for an adhesive or wedge anchor
Suction or adhesive dust cupsA cup that sticks to the wall around the bit and catches falling dustDrywall and light-duty holes; a supplement to extraction, not a substitute for it in masonry

HEPA vacuum and damp wipe cleanupVacuuming holes, sills and floor with a HEPA vac, then wiping surfaces with damp clothsEvery job, at every hole, before the panel goes up and again before leaving

Two methods that come up and should not be used indoors: wet drilling next to finished floors and occupied spaces (the slurry is harder to contain than the dust) and blowing out holes with compressed air or a bulb (it puts the finest dust straight into the room).

How We Plan an Occupied-Building Install

  1. Walk the space with facilities. Identify the wall material, what is on the other side of the wall, and whether the area is patient care, public, or back-of-house.
  2. Get the permits in hand. In healthcare that means the ICRA permit and any hot work or above-ceiling permits. In offices and schools it is usually a work authorization and an agreed schedule.
  3. Lay out and mark every hole first. Template the whole gallery before the drill comes out so each hole is drilled once, in the right spot. On brick, choose standoff positions that land on solid brick or consistently in the mortar, so the anchors hold and no hole has to be redrilled.
  4. Coordinate around smoke detectors. Never cover or disable a detector on your own. If the facility wants detectors protected or the zone placed on test, they arrange it and set up any fire watch the building requires.
  5. Drill with extraction on every hole. Shroud and HEPA extractor for masonry, with the extractor running before the bit touches the wall and a few seconds after it leaves.
  6. Vacuum the hole, set the anchor, hang the panel. A clean hole also makes a stronger anchor, so dust control and mounting quality go together.
  7. Leave it cleaner than we found it. HEPA-vacuum the floor, baseboards and ledges, damp wipe, bag the debris before it leaves the area, and walk the finished wall with the facility contact.

Scheduling Around the Building

Even with good capture, drilling is loud. In hospitals and clinics we schedule the drilling for the lowest-traffic hours the facility allows, often early mornings, evenings or weekends, and do the quiet work of hanging and leveling panels afterward. In offices, drilling before business hours and hanging during the day keeps the disruption to a minimum. For a large gallery, splitting the job into zones keeps any one corridor open.

Quick Answers

Do I need an ICRA permit just to hang signs in a hospital? Usually yes, if the work involves drilling. The infection prevention team decides the class; many signage jobs fall into the lower classes and take a few minutes to permit.

Is a shop vac good enough? Not for masonry dust in an occupied building. A standard shop vac filter passes the fine particles straight back into the room. Use a HEPA-rated extractor or vacuum.

Can adhesive mounting avoid the dust entirely? Sometimes. Lightweight acrylic signs and vinyl can go up with adhesive or mounting tape on smooth surfaces. Heavier panels, standoffs, and textured brick like the wall in the photo above need mechanical anchors to be safe.

Does dust control add much cost? Very little. The equipment is part of how we work, and planning the layout carefully usually saves more time than the extraction setup takes.
